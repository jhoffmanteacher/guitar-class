📖The Storywhere it came from & what to hear▾

Quick history: Dylan wrote it in 1967 for his stripped-down album "John Wesley Harding" — three short verses, no chorus, over in two and a half minutes. Jimi Hendrix heard it within weeks and rebuilt it completely; his version became his biggest American hit, and Dylan liked it so much he started playing the song Hendrix's way. Listen closely and you'll hear a second guitar under everything: a 12-string acoustic strumming the whole ride. And the covers kept coming: in 1992 Neil Young tore through it at Dylan's 30th-anniversary concert at Madison Square Garden — raw, stomping, no wah in sight — and that performance is what this page's play-along loop is built from.

What to hear — a listening map for your first pass through the Hendrix recording:

The first seconds — the whole song arrives at once: the Am · G · F · G loop, hit hard. Count it before anything else happens.
Two guitars — Hendrix's electric out front, and a 12-string acoustic strumming underneath from start to finish. Try to hear them separately.
Between the vocal lines — the lead guitar answers every line the voice sings. That conversation is what Layer 6 teaches.
The big solo — one solo with several personalities in a row: clean melody, then sliding notes, then the wah pedal making the guitar "talk". Count the sections as they change.
The last verse — the title line ("All along the watchtower…") doesn't arrive until the final verse. Notice how the band lifts when it lands.
The third version — this page's play-along loop comes from Neil Young's 1992 performance: one raw guitar, no wah, pure stomp. Find it and compare — the same three chords, a third personality.

This song exists twice: Bob Dylan's 1967 original and Jimi Hendrix's 1968 cover — same three chords, very different guitars. Before you play a note, hear what one arrangement choice (acoustic vs. electric) does to the same song.

Do it: listen to the opening minute of both versions. One guitar is acoustic, one is electric — decide which is which, and name one word for how each version feels.
Respond: Dylan wrote it — so why do most people know the Hendrix version? What did the cover add? Answer in a frame: "The Hendrix version is the famous one because ___."
Connect: name another song where a cover became more famous than the original — "___ is better known than the original."
Check it when: you've answered the questions and can hum the main hook from memory.

Bonus layer — optional harder challenge, no score. Best once Layer 4's pentatonic box feels automatic.

Hendrix never just strums this song — between vocal lines, his guitar answers the singer with short fills (a fill is a short extra run between main parts). This one lives entirely in the Pattern 1 box you learned in Layer 4: three notes, played as one question-and-answer phrase over the loop. Every note is already under your fingers. Three parts below: the answer, the entrance, and the climb — all from the same box.

Part B: slide up the D string from fret 5 into fret 7 — pick once, glide, land on A and let it sing two beats. Then answer: C (G string, fret 5), D (fret 7), C again, and home to A (D string, fret 7), ringing until the loop restarts.

Part C: bar 1 climbs the box a note at a time: A, C, D, E. Bar 2 leaps to the peak and falls home: high C (high e, fret 8), A, G (B string, fret 8), and land on A. This is the shape of a Hendrix ending — up to the peak, then home.
Stuck?
Loop just the string cross — A (D string, fret 7) to C (G string, fret 5) and back — until it's automatic, then add the rest one note at a time.
Level up
Play it the way Hendrix uses it: strum a lap of the Layer 5 loop, drop in Part A, then go back to strumming. Open your next lap with Part B, and save Part C for the big finish. Even better with a partner — one strums Dylan, one answers as Hendrix, then trade. Or write your own answer: keep bar 1, and invent a new bar 2 from any Pattern 1 notes that lands on A.

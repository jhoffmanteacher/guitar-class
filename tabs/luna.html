More about this song

Peso Pluma and Junior H's "Luna" is one of the biggest hits of its time in the sierreño style (a modern Mexican acoustic style), sung over just an acoustic requinto and bass — which is why every layer here builds toward playing it acoustic, without an amplifier.

All layers are teaching arrangements — simplified for learning, not note-for-note transcriptions. Tempo targets stay on the usual steps — 60, then 70, then 80 downbeat BPM — even though the original recording moves faster in 6/8.

This track runs at record speed (128 BPM), felt in 2 — same two-downbeat-strums-per-bar feel you've been playing since Module 3, just at the record's real tempo.

Before you play a note, feel where the pulse lives. "Luna" is in 6/8 — count it out and you might land on 2 big beats or 6 small ones, and both are true at once: two big beats, each one dividing into three. Tap along with just the two big beats and let the requinto play the rest.

Do it: listen to the first verse. Tap only the two big downbeats per bar — try not to tap all six.
Respond: what does the requinto add to the song that strumming alone can't? Answer in a frame: "The requinto adds ___."
Connect: name another song where an acoustic guitar carries the hook (the catchy part you remember), not just the rhythm — "In ___, the acoustic guitar carries the hook."
Check it when: you've answered the questions and can hum the main hook from memory.

Hammer-ons and pull-offs
Same box, two new moves. A hammer-on (h): pick the first note, then tap the higher fret hard with your ring finger — two notes, one pick. A slide (/): pick once, keep pressing, glide up. Bonus layer — optional harder challenge, no score. Best once Layer 5's open chords feel steady.

The requinto doesn't just open the song — it answers the singer all the way through, weaving its lead line around the same chord shapes you strummed in Layer 5. This layer teaches the whole lead in four parts: Part A lives down low, inside your chord shapes, and Parts B–D follow the requinto up past fret 12 for the song's bridge — the highest ground you've played all year.

Part A — verse answers. Keep the chord shape held down and pick single notes out of it — every note is already under your fingers.

Play it: hold the little F for bars 1–2 — pick C (B string, fret 1), A (G string, fret 2), and land on F (D string, fret 3). Switch to Am for bars 3–4 — pick the open high e (E), C, and land on A. Notes fall on the big beats at 60 BPM; let each one ring into the next.

Part B — home base at fret 12. For the song's bridge, the requinto jumps up the neck. Slide your hand up until your index finger sits at fret 12 on the high e string — that's your new home. The whole phrase is a rock back and forth: pick fret 12, slide up to 13 without letting off pressure, then come back and let 12 ring. Those notes are E and F — the same two notes as your open high e and fret 1, one octave up.

Part C — the climb to fret 17. The bridge peaks on a drumroll of repeated notes at fret 17 — a high A, your highest note of the year — then falls: 18, 13, slide down to 12, back home. Count the drumroll with the full 6/8 count — 1-2-3-4-5-6, one pick per number. Use two small home spots: index at fret 17 (middle finger grabs 18), then hop your index down to 13 and slide it into 12.

Play it: Parts B and C single-note first, big beats at 60 BPM — the rock, then the climb and the fall — until you can run the whole bridge line twice without stopping.

Part D — the sierreño sixths. Here's the signature sierreño sound. The requinto doesn't play the bridge melody alone — it doubles every note with a partner note below it on the G string, both ringing together (double stops). Look at the pairs: 17 with 17, 18 with 19, 13 with 14, 12 with 12. Easiest way to sound both: the pick hits the G string while your middle finger plucks the high e at the same moment.

Play it: add the G-string partners, slow — two clean pairs in a row before you chain the phrase. When the sixths ring clean, that's the record's sound coming off your guitar.
Stuck?
Part A: strum the chord once, then pick its notes one at a time without lifting any finger — a dead note means a neighboring finger is brushing that string; arch your knuckles. Parts B–D: past fret 12 the frets get skinny — stand your fingertips up taller and land right behind the fret wire. If a slide dies halfway, you let off pressure: keep pressing the whole ride.
Level up
Stack the layers: open with the Layer 6 roll, play Part A over a verse, hit the bridge in sixths, then land back on the Layer 5 vamp — that's the whole song's shape under your hands. Even better with a partner: one strums, one leads, then trade.
